Chapter 10


“Move over, sea salt. We have homo salt!”

The soil was, even then, lone beyond the hills. Without greens or rainbows of the ground. Yet in the row of townhouses, there were trees. One for each house, and it towered over. But they were all unclothed. Branches stuck out like spikes on a medieval morning star. The wood was ash coloured, and peeling.
“It is normal in the fall for a tree to look cold. I reckon it could still be kicking,” Aric said.
“It’s summer,” Avalon said, walking to the tree.
“I know it is,” he mumbled before catching up to her.
She placed her unsoiled hand on the trunk, feeling as little hope as she’d had for the soil. Then, she frowned; her temple creased in exactly three lines.
“Nothing again. Odd. I thought trees were known for their resilience and longevity.”
“That is what I heard too,” he replied, shrugging.
She pulled off her hand. It’d been tarnished already. She rubbed fingertip against fingertip, toying with the white powder that stuck to them. “Mildew. It may seem that the tree is rotting from the inside.”
“No one on this isle will want to make acquaintance with us; however, in the chance they do, please, do not shake their hands. They’ll throw daggers at us.”
Avalon put out her hands in front. “What a start. Two hands soiled and not a single lead.”
“Like you said, it’s only the start. Who’d expect you to resolve this complex matter in so little time?”
“You. That’s the first thing you asked when we arrived.”
“I wasn’t serious. That was my attempt at comedy. Now, I realize there is no place for comedy in this ruin.”
“Ruin…” she blew out a breath. “It already seems unlikely we’ll find life beyond the humans who inhabit this land; still, I want to do all I can. I don’t want to believe that an isle can go barren out of the blue. For even the blue comes from somewhere.”
“I want to do all we can too. It’d be a terrible precedent. Or worse, it could spread.” He brushed up a fragment of wood that’d been hanging off. “If this ruin is unnatural, we will find the cause. I promise that we will.”
How easy it’d been to allay her concerns, knowing they were both on the same page. While he was often humorous—and it rubbed on her—he didn’t mention the bounty this time around, and that showed her he did care.
“Now, come on.” He slung an arm over her shoulder. “Let’s rinse these hands in the ocean before we have to shake any hands.”
She'd thought of removing his arm. She didn't. He meant no harm, and he wasn't plague ridden.

The water was alive. It’s always been for Avalon. She gasped as she plunged a hand and the other.
Aric thought the water must’ve been cold. He dipped his finger, but the water was lukewarm.
Meanwhile, for Avalon, water pecked at her skin like gentle kisses. They bounced off the back of her hand, while the water under her palm seemed to have a mass. As she rubbed her hands together, that mass dissolved. The water moved as though it wanted to be heard by her.
“The sea is very much alive. It’s almost overwhelming for someone like me to plunge a hand in it, let alone my body.” She shook her head. “That one is tough. Every organism has a presence; and they course through me with such intensity. I’m not sure what I’m meant to do with this. And why I, of all people, was bestowed with this additional sense. As science and medicine advance, tools and machines will do what I can do—as accurately. Hence, why? Why the ability? Why me; a human?”
She yanked her hands out and shook off as much water as she could.
“See, that’s interesting. The sea feels alive to me also. For different reasons, I’m sure. Imagination is one. Existentialism, another. There are many things I wonder, and at night, when it’s time to surrender to sleep, I try to find peace in knowing the answers to those perpetually hanging questions wouldn’t change a thing.
“Whether we are here for a purpose or not doesn’t render us more or less significant.” He straightened up. “I’m not sure what I’m saying, really. I have many questions; as you do. I live with wonder, but try not to place that too high.”
Avalon flustered and picked up her dress by the hem, then she roughly wiped her hands with it. “I don’t place my wonder too high either. It’s the water that’s made me maudlin. Water’s often associated with emotions. I read that in tarot books. The cards with water in the background or in cups usually relate to emotions.”
“You forgot tears; they’re emotional and water,” he remarked with a nod.
“And they’re as salty as the ocean,” she added begrudgingly—because it was right there.
“Move over, sea salt. We have homo salt!” he declared to the ocean.
She turned her head to grin, both amused and grateful he’d diverted the topic—with humour no less. She had to press her lips to suppress laughter. That had never happened before. Nothing or no one had made her want to laugh so much she’d needed to conceal it. Reed was the funniest person she knew, and the man’s forte was deadpan sarcasm. What exuded from her then was agreement, and a pat on the back—if he’d been extra pointy.
“Hey, do you hear a sound? A strange one.” Aric probed, stirring back to the hills.
She pressed her hands against her knees to stand, and quickly, the need for laughter had dissipated. She had heard it. “There is something. Something akin to wailing.”
Aric looked back, perplexed. “Wailing? I think it’s closer to a chant.”
They crossed the hills again, and surely, there was a chant. One so filled with sorrow that Avalon’s definition was not far off.
The haunting recite, not far off in the distance, had given Aric chills. “I don’t do well with ghosts. I’m ready to return to Evird. Unlike you, I care little about professionalism.”
She pulled on his sleeve. “They’re weeping, but in song. Their voices harmonize so well, I mistook them for one.”
“And I, for ghosts. I could still be right on that one.”
“Let’s follow the voices,” she said, taking the lead.
“We don’t need to,” he said, his lips dry. “We’re looking for greens which haven’t fully succumbed to death yet. Ghosts couldn’t be deader.”
She eyed him fiercely, both startled and bothered by his display of fright. “Aric, you don’t seriously believe that ghosts, in broad daylight, would parade their existence for everyone to hear. They’re probably prayers for their land. Listen more closely. Try to make out what they’re singing about because I can’t. I’ve never been good at recognizing operatic syllables.”
“And you think I’m good?” he cocked a brow. “I am actually. My parents insisted I be cultured and follow them to the theatre every so often. Alright, I’ll listen closer.”
With that, they’d continued to walk toward the haunting sonance.
Avalon had also gone to the theatre as much as every so often, yet the choice of plays, musicals, or symphonies wasn’t hers. Hence, she didn’t feel like enjoying what she’d been obliged to consume.
“Father, oh, father, I can hear. Heal our mother nature, I can also hear, which is ironic since mother nature is the healer. And now I can’t tell if they’re repeating from the plague or from the vague, because vague in French is wave; that could be fitting too.”
“Well, isn’t that vague,” she huffed.
“This is no laughing matter,” he teased.
“It isn’t, but you are. Of course it’s plague. At least now you can understand they’re no ghosts.”
“Are you sure?” He pointed to a tree, one seemingly still kicking, with vines draping down loose. “They sure look it.” Women, clothed in white gowns so long they reached the ground, their hems soiled. They circled the tree, hands intertwined. Their fervent voices had been the ones echoing in the forest.
She crossed her arms. “Sure, they might resemble ghosts from the books, but I still don’t believe that they are.”
He scoffed, “Your case is weak. Long black hair, long white gowns, eerie sound.”
“One of them has a blond bob. And did you not see the redhead? Your point is moot.”
He licked his lips and tucked some hair behind his ear. “Now…how are we going to get to that tree? Should we go back to the boat and wait until they’re done?”
“We’ll wait here. I’m telling you, they’re harmless human beings.”
“Alright now, they might be human, but you can’t be sure they’re harmless. You know of their reputation.”
“I almost forgot about that,” she said pensive.
“So we bolt?”
“We hold.”
Aric had swiftly given up—because it was mostly a farce. He didn’t believe they were ghosts, despite their obvious appearance. It was all for banter. He thought her effervescent; as a tablet. Dropped in water and she’d implode in a controlled manner. The glass is full of her as she swims in his mind, words bubbling, expressions captured. He hadn’t had this much entertainment seeking a bounty in what seemed like an eternity.
“Hey, there’s a man too. He’s watching them from behind, with a bouquet,” Avalon said.
“Could he be the cult leader, and what he’s holding: the offering?”
“Compared to your ghost theory, this one is more plausible.”
“I was jesting.”
The man, dressed in a tight fit ivory blouse and complementary ivory trousers, had swivelled his head ever so slightly in their direction. Both quieted down, struck by his kind regard. His blue eyes, his golden hair, neatly going down his nape, and thin bangs skimming his forehead like sheer curtains. He stretched the corners of his lips upwards with slow demeanour—one could call that grace—he then nodded once before swivelling back to face the choir.
“Maybe they’re not all against us,” she murmured, dazzled. The man appeared as they described Prince Charming in the classic tales.
“Didn’t you say my cult theory was plausible? In that case, this is how they get you.” Aric, for one, was dubious already. He thought the ivory attire and the golden hair emitted enough warmth to compensate for a lack of.
“We’re not here long enough to build rapport with a cult.”
“Again, that’s what you think. Fortunately, I’m here and won’t let that happen to you. I’ll keep you grounded if necessary.”
To that remark, she tore her gaze off Prince Charming and sank her chin at Aric. “Weren’t you jesting about the cult theory?” She, too, sought to rile him up when opportunity presented itself. Aric had spoken cordially about everyone, whether he’d known them personally or not. His sudden suspicions for the man piqued her interest. It was a mask off his face, and another uncovered. She longed to see the jester turn serious. Aric would be the one to satiate her appetite for perspectives if she provoked him further.
“I’d so like to talk to him. He might be the only gentle soul in Gardenia. What do you think, Aric?” She batted her eyelashes.
