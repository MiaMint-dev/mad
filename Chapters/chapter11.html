Chapter 11


"God?"

“He still doesn’t look like a cult leader to you?” Aric asked after the blond man dressed in ivory walked to the weeping tree. The women had ended their chant with a long note, and he’d gone to murmur to them.
“Talking to women now means you’re a cult leader?” suggested Avalon. “Envy’s the poison in your veins,” she told Aric, having the fun she swore she’d have with this matter.
He scoffed. “It’s not envy. It’s fishy. He is.”
As the choir dispersed, the man stood witnessing the tree weeping with vines, and the haunting chant was swiftly replaced by the vines swaying under the influence of the wind. He set down the bouquet; lilies neatly cut to even length. Not a second had passed before the man turned to them again. He offered, most unambiguously to Avalon, a dashing smile. She was pleased by his attention, for she was unrestricted. In a land she might never see again. Where people won’t talk about a woman, which no one had affiliation with. In the company of people who’d become a tale to tell and no more.
Aric, on the other hand, was curious. As he walked to them, he thought the man offbeat. Deceitful. Ill-intentioned. Of course, his mere instincts were voicing those descriptions. Instincts which he had even trust in. As even as tangible proof.
“Hello,” the man uttered in a voice which was somehow both robust and vibrant. Avalon compared it to roaring thunder, which, on stormy nights, she found comforting. Aric compared it to the aftermath of a lightning strike.
“Welcome to Gardenia,” he added.
Avalon linked her hands behind her back. “Thank you. Um, we didn’t mean to interrupt. We simply felt pulled to the sound.” She raised the tone of her voice by half a notch and gracefully spaced out her words.
The man’s smile grew wider. “You haven’t interrupted anything,” he said. “We rarely ever receive visitors anymore. I, for one, believe we hold all responsibility for it. And God is sending us a clear message of his disapproval.”
Aric snapped up at the name. “God?” He did not believe in the name or its representation.
The man pursued: “Word spread in Gardenia that God is punishing our nation for our, um, overbearingness. It may be the only explanation for this senseless devastation; it seems.”
Aric shot a glance at Avalon. He burned to remind her of his cult theory. He bore through her face, hoping she’d respond to his telepathy. She didn’t glance back at him, though she noticed his lit eyes from the corner of hers.
“It seems?” she probed. “Are there other conjectures?”
“Like this is a reasonable conjecture,” Aric mumbled quietly, his lips barely moving.
Avalon then shot him the glance he waited for, or at least thought he was, for she cussed with her eyes. telepathy went through.
The man chuckled, bringing back Avalon’s attention to him.
“It is hard to believe that God could be so cruel to his children. Yes. I, myself, was dubious at first. But it remains most plausible when thinking of the damage we’ve done to Gardenia. Closing it as so did not render us the self-sustaining isle we thought to be.” He sighed. “If you’d like to hear more; I’d be happy to invite you to my workplace: The Gardenia’s Choice Floral Shop. Or the dainty coffee shop next to it. Oh, and my name is Anton Verne. Apologies it took so long to introduce myself.”
The man reached a hand out to Avalon as he trailed the motions on her face. The slight rise of her lips. The faint flutter of her lashes. The coyness in her eyes.
“Avalon Gone,” she returned, taking his hand. His skin was coarse, yet the surface of his palm was soft and without a callous.
“I’ve never encountered an Avalon before. The name suits you beautifully.” His eyes glinted as he said it.
“We have business to attend to,” Aric blurted out. “Perhaps we’ll swing by, were we stranded here any longer.”
“And you are?” The man turned to Aric, as he hadn’t for a while.
“Aric. Aric, the bounty hunter,” he answered because it’s all he thought the man needed to know.
“I sure hope you won’t feel as though you’re stranded here.” He laughed by himself. “Do come by anytime you’d like. Perhaps I won’t be at the shop late at night, but I’m often at the pub, coincidentally in the same street. You can easily find me here, or anyone. Say the name, and everyone will have the answer.”
“I’m sure we will find you,” replied Avalon.
“Then I’ll be expecting you.”
He gave Aric a nod and Avalon a smile with closed lips. She watched Anton leave, and Aric watched her watch him leave.
“See, I don’t think he is a cult leader. I’m sure you’ve noticed that those don’t have a regal track record of handsomeness. Which begs the question: How can one fall victim to a grotesque looking man in the first place?”
“Charm is an easy quality. Anyone can have charm. Deceptively so,” he said firmly.
“Are you calling him deceptive?”
“I’d have to call him charming first,” he quipped.
“Fair enough.”
In the absence of footsteps within earshot, they believed themselves to now be alone. Left only with the wind, which blew at them petulantly.
“The tree’s free to use,” he remarked.
“Don’t say use.” Her words came out too soft to think she was upset.
“It’s free to engage with,” he rectified, motioning to the tree with a serving hand.
“That’s better.” She grinned. “Let us try this out.”
“You don’t have to tell me twice—even if I had to.”
Avalon could not muster a laugh at his wit. The time for pleasantries had depended on a switch. With a flip, it switched.
“I have an odd feeling about this one too,” she said.
“Even when it’s so green?”
“Even when it’s so green,” she sighed.
Avalon took measured steps to the tree. She sensed it weeping beyond its appearance. The faintest sobs raised the fuzz on the back of her neck. She’d learned to discern a tree’s calling from the simple breeze by spending ample time in the woods. The woods, to her, were bustling. Everything, from a sprout to a monstrous tree, lived to tell a tale. A tale of stillness, and from it, witness. The natural forces in Gardenia were much quieter than she’d been used to. But in the absence of sensation was undeniably the presence of its counterpart; the destroyer. Avalon had been sure of it as she placed her hands on the tree. She leaned forward to press even the side of her face against the rough wood.
“Careful there. It could graze your skin,” Aric said, wincing.
“It won’t hurt me,” she hummed. “Think of what I’m sensing as a heartbeat,” she started—to not leave Aric in the dark. “And there is a heartbeat.”
He could not smile about it when her lips dwelt in a line.
“It is alive, then,” he whispered, uncertain.
“It is. But the heartbeat is sporadic. It walks, then it runs, then it halts, and when you think it’s no longer there; it beats again. In all its motion, the feeling is feeble. That, from every direction.”
“Even when it runs?” he asked as he pressed his back against the trunk. Though he did not have Avalon’s added flair, he was human and sought out nature’s warm maternity.
“It’s feeblest when it runs, because that’s when the pain strikes.”
“That’s horrible,” he said, looking up to the sky.
“Don’t feel too bad. Trees feel pain much differently from us. Matter of fact, they don’t. They perceive and can react to change. They are sensitive to light and weather, yet they lack the brain and nerves to feel. Their communications help me more than they do them.”
“Oh.” He paused. “Somehow, that helps curb my own sadness. Can it? Should it?”
“Of course.”
“Does it bother your connection if I lay against the tree?”
“No.”
Aric hadn’t said a word after, leaving her to concentrate. He closed his eyes; breathing soundly; waiting.
Whilst he saw darkness, she saw little lines with the semblance of veins all crossing offhandedly. There wasn’t really a heartbeat, but more so a pulse. It was a maze of veins. If she’d focus on a vein, she’d find a smaller one inside. They appeared endless. Avalon could only differentiate them by their luminosity and pulse intensity. Certain were nearly blinding, while others, hard to see. And everything in between dimmed and shone, respectively. She sleuthed through the pallid-looking ones, reasoning they’d be actively fighting the toxins.
Little time after, she exhaled loudly as she tore away from the trunk. Aric startled, he’d been at peace, simply waiting with closed eyes.
“I think I’ll be here a while,” she said. “There’s an array of leads. But I feel the trouble is deeper than the eye can see. Good thing I don’t use my eyes. Though it still means I’ll have a thorny time.”
“It’s alright. I can wait.” He peeked at her, eyes relaxed.
Her face was pained when she told him: “I’d rather be alone.” Then she faltered, a little sorry. “Maybe you could go see Anton? And collect information out of him.”
Aric startled once more and withdrew his weight from the tree.
"What could we possibly need from that man? You said so yourself that we're not here long enough to build rapport with a cult, and that's all I'd be doing if I went."
She looked at him blankly. "Are you vulnerable?"
"No."
"Cults prey on the vulnerable. Anton, if he is part of a cult, won't prey on you. But if he does, by some chance, it's information. Wouldn't you like to be useful too? Wouldn't you like to earn your part of the bounty?"
He pulled a wry face. "Does it have to be that guy?"
"Do as you please, but he sure did seem to know a lot, and that would sure narrow your search." If there’d been anything dubious about Anton, she trusted Aric would uncover it—with his prejudice and wit. "I'll come find you as soon as I can, and we'll leave," she assured him.
"It's for you that I’ll do this," he breathed.
Then he took off.
Aric didn’t intend to leave for long. He didn’t need her to find him. He’d be back before she could try—if things went smoothly; over no more than a single espresso. And he’d come back sincere, with information rather than an excuse.
Avalon sat on her dress, beside the tree, with both hands pinned on each side. She placed them at the very bottom. It was as close as could get to the roots. And as though something had taken over her, she stilled; like the tree itself. She became unbothered by the chills passing through her body from every gust of wind. And sounds had quite literally passed through one ear and out the other.
When somebody walked to her, she didn’t notice. That until he whispered in her ear: “Are you a tree doctor?”
