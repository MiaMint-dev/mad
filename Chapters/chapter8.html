Chapter 8


The future was yesterday

Avalon returned to Aric his jacket, that, despite his reluctance—for the morning cold had vanished under the sun. She shoved it into his bag. He didn’t feel petty enough to take the jacket out and for her to repeat the act, then, him. He worried again. That’s all he could do. Avalon was oblivious to his worries, because she didn’t consider he would; or that anyone would. The concerns she held, she thought, were well hidden. She’d no clue he thought as she did. In her mind, it was every man for himself. He was no less than a stranger still.
“We can see Foem Island now,” he said, and she flew to the edge of the boat. She squinted her eyes; the horizon was much too vast and the closest land too narrow.
“It seems awfully far.”
He clicked his tongue. “No, no, that is hope. A speck in the sea is always hope. Don’t despair at its sight. Rejoice. We’re almost there.”
Avalon opened her eyes to his perspective, that of a sailor, and thought to correct her words. “So close, yet so far.”
He stared at her for a second. “Maybe.”
Aric had been faithful to the concept of hope. As a sailor, he had to. With lengthy isolation eventually came introspection and questions for the universe. He spoke them aloud. He looked to find meaning in what surrounded him. He personified the sea and befriended its entity. ‘It’s breathing,’ he would say at the sight of waves crashing and pulling away from the sand. He thought it similar to how the living inhaled and exhaled. That was proof enough to him that the vibrant sea had life. That it could feel and think like any intelligent being. It perhaps held more complex thoughts and profound knowledge.
“Could you tell me more about Foem Island?”
He joined her to the edge of the boat in prudent strides.
“Foem Island is, well, small. It’s just as vast as your town. For a very long time, they’ve prided themselves on being a self-reliant and independent nation. Now, uh, they’ve found themselves wounded in pride. All considering that they’ve had to request assistance and negotiate outside deals. They were more so an exporting country rather than an importing one. They’re really struggling with this bee shortage. It’s horrific how they’re all dropping like flies—or bees.” He winced; the comedy had written itself. But it was no laughing matter.
“The more imports they have to rely on, the less local business becomes relevant. And that causes a degrading economy, and in due course, inflation. Things had been so great for that little nation.”
“What was Foem Island before this ordeal, do you know?”
“Of course I know,” he sighed. “It’s the greenest island I know. You’d expect palm trees and fallen coconuts; instead, there are sheep and fruitful bushes everywhere. The islanders are um…wary of outsiders but have an utmost kindness for their own. Don’t let that frighten you. They’re just unfamiliar with other people.”
“How so?”
“They’re not quite the tourist island. It’s barely a pit stop for sailors; they’d rather throw their anchor out in a grander land.”
“What made you stop there?”
“I’m no regular sailor. I indiscriminately visit every speck of land I happen to catch from afar. But I’d visited the island before I sailed out on my own. We’d recreationally sail the world as a family. Every summer. That is how I grew to love wandering. You could say my wanderlust was passed on to me by my family. My father, my mother, my sister, and my brother.”
“You have a brother too. There must have never been a dull day in your childhood.” Aric had been living her dream, and she grew curious about him for it. She pictured a tight knit family. They laughed together at the one who was miserably losing their round of blackjack. Another. Aric passed the butter to his sister while his brother dropped freshly toasted bread on her plate. His father downed his coffee and walked to the wheel, his eyes out to the horizon. His mother was smiling, content with how her sons cared for their sister. Comfort and envy mingled in her chest, neither willing to make space.
“There was never a dull day, no,” he said pointedly. “Going back to Foem Island. I’d been there once at the age of 15. And once more, merely days ago. Its appearance, I’d say, hasn’t changed. I mean, it’s a bee shortage, not a greenery loss. The morale is much lower, though. People are losing hope for their nation. And they’re angry. Angry that they’ve no choice but to turn to others. They fear they’re losing their identity this way.”
“Are they? Or is it a fear born from thoughts? How big an impact does it make to source honey from neighbouring nations?” Her question had not been born from dismissal, but simply from a craving for understanding. She’d been blunt; but she was fortunate to be conversing with Aric, who craved discussion all the same.
“It is a fear born from thoughts and ideals. Some think of it as karma. Not the islanders themselves; their neighbours. Their adamancy for self-reliance had turned them to isolation. They entertained outsiders—with pride for their country—yet couldn’t accept the one who fell for it. Their doors are windows. You may look, but you may not come in. They’re simply very traditional.”
She glared at him. “That’s not traditional; that’s possibly offensive. Are they protective of their land, or critical of foreigners? One could have a foot in nativism and another in chauvinism. Depending on what more you can tell me, I’ll have no choice but to be prejudiced against them—as they are against me.”
He beamed with amusement, his eyes wide for a second or two.
“There might be a little of both, yes. The collective is protective and preservative, considering that they’ve worked arduously to build and maintain their community. They’re more afraid than hateful. It’s the greenest place you’ll ever see. It’s a gem. A beautiful card castle, my father called it. They’re afraid of the wind. One gust and it’s ruined.”
“You know, controlling people often end up being parents to rebellious children,” she said, a hint of a smile crossing her face.
“Are you speaking from experience?” he guessed, eager to learn more about her. There was more to her. Behind the cold front and the indifference. Something gleamed in the distance. Who was to know of her true colours?
“I am not traditionally rebellious. No. I’ve kept many secrets, not to harm anyone, but to escape a future built for me. I don’t believe one’s future can be sourced from a textbook.
“I am a rebellious child, only because I am their child. A product of their night and a notion of control passed down from generations.” She stepped back, startled by her admittance. She shouldn’t have said this much. Yet she did and couldn’t swallow back the words. They lingered, sprawled in the air, inhaled by his ears.
“Well, rebellious child, what kind of secrets did you keep? Am I stepping on the line? Do you still need to keep these secrets? Without their presence, are you still the rebellious child?”
She stood close to the edge again. “You crossed a line, yes. But my secrets are no longer. That, too, is a question of perspective. I haven’t committed a crime, yet I might as well have in their eyes—if they’d known. Gone are their eyes, and so is their label on me.”
Aric found she spoke almost in riddles. Some people, such as himself, answered in straight lines, while some others, such as Avalon, spouted amorphous figures.
“What kind of child are you now? Perhaps you aren’t a child anymore.”
She inhaled soundly. No longer was she going to be treated as a precious child. For better and worse. She was on the cusp of rebirth, for the first had appeared fruitless. “I’m not,” she drawled with poise and subtle mirth.
She looked at Aric, feeling smaller and colder, as her new reality began to set. The absence of an old life had always been the presence of a new one. She lived thinking the future was yesterday, and what she’d dreamed of would be a blink away, behind her.
“I’m not,” she repeated. “I’m born again, under the sun and moon, over the sea, on this very boat.” She laughed at her own words. “I’m sorry. I don’t mean to be philosophical. I am not.”
He turned and pressed his back against the railing, but his head was angled toward her. “It’s alright,” he nodded. “I am not as pensive on land as I am on board. There’s much space for silence and slowness here.”
Then followed the exact silence he referred to. Waves had long since become white noise. They were alone at sea. Without a crowd or a mere passerby. Time seemed irrelevant. As did everything else. Life or death could find them without notice or a witness. The quiet had swamped them as it unburdened them.
